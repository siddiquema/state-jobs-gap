The post's three questions

How many jobs have been created?

The closest public measure is new formal-sector payroll from EPFO, split by age. It counts new subscribers minus exits plus rejoiners, so it is not a count of jobs created, and it leaves out informal and government work.

When do people join?

Company joining dates are not public. EPFO publishes monthly totals by state and age band, which is as fine as the public record goes.

How many migrations will be stopped?

No target has been found, but budget speeches and state policies have not been searched yet. The data below shows how far graduate output and formal entry differ, and why that difference cannot be read as people who left.

Reading the gap: what the raw difference contains

Pass-outs, less those who continue to a master's (proxied by postgraduate pass-outs), less graduates outside the labour force and the unemployed, give employed graduates. Formal entrants are set beside that figure. Millions of people over five years; illustrative.

CaseEmployed graduatesFormal entrantsRemainder
  • Place. Pass-outs are counted by the institution's state; EPFO assigns workers to states by a method it does not state. Students, graduates and workers cross state lines.
  • Age. Pass-outs are graduates of any age; payroll entrants are aged 18 to 25 and include people who are not graduates; population is aged 18 to 23.
  • Kind. Payroll is a flow of new entrants. The household survey used to check it counts a stock of people in work.
  • Period. Academic years run July to June, financial years April to March, and graduates mostly join the year after.

The remainder could be informal or government work, formal entrants who are not graduates, people who moved in from other states, or graduates working in other states. This data cannot separate them.

What this does not show

  • The gap is not a migration count. It also holds graduates who study further, work informally or for government, start businesses, are unemployed or are outside the labour force.
  • EPFO does not state how it assigns workers to states. If it uses the employer's registered state, a West Bengal graduate employed by a firm registered elsewhere counts there. A check against survey data counted where people live reproduced the same order of states, so attribution is unlikely to create the pattern, but it may still inflate head-office states a little.
  • AISHE pass-outs for 2023-24 fall sharply, and institution response rates do not explain it. Totals over five years are safer than any single year.
  • PLFS counts people where they live now, so it cannot show departures. A low unemployment rate says nothing about job quality: self-employed and casual workers count as employed.
  • EPFO figures for recent months are provisional; earlier annual figures did not change between releases.

Worth pondering beyond the three questions

Inferences from the data, not findings. Each could be wrong.

  • The constraint looks like formal job entry, not graduate supply. On EPFO's measure, matching the lowest peer's formal entry per young person would mean about — times today's formal entrants; on the residence-based check it is about — to — times. Even if every graduate stayed, current formal hiring could not absorb them, so a target to stop migration is mainly a job-creation target.
  • Job quality may matter as much as job count. Regular wage or salaried work is — of West Bengal's workers, against — to — in the high-entry states, and — of its regular non-farm jobs carry no social security, against — to —. There are fewer such jobs and more of them are informal.
  • Women graduates are the largest unused pool. Women are the majority of undergraduate pass-outs, but far fewer of them are in the labour force than men. The pattern is national, yet it explains more of the shortfall than migration can.
  • Vocational training looks like a weak link. Unemployment among diploma and certificate holders in West Bengal is well above India's, as the chart above shows.
  • The people who migrate most may not be graduates. A graduate-focused question can miss lower-skilled out-migration, which migration surveys would show better.
  • Kolkata looks like a low-wage, low-unemployment city. If so, pay and productivity matter more than counting jobs. Its average regular pay is among the lowest of the million-plus cities.
  • The question can be answered directly. EPFO accounts are linked to Aadhaar and AISHE records pass-outs by institution. A graduate tracer study (where graduates work, for how much, in which state) would replace most of these inferences.
  • A three-number yearly scorecard would make any claim testable: formal entry per young person, graduate unemployment, and median graduate pay.
  • Still unchecked: the mix of pass-outs (general degrees against professional courses, regular against distance), and factory employment from the new Annual Survey of Industries.
